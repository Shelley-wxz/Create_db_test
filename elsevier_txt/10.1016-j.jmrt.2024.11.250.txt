Title: Investigation on oxidation behavior and mechanical properties of FeCoNiNbAlCrTa high entropy alloy at 900 °C
DOI: 10.1016/j.jmrt.2024.11.250
Publication Name: Journal of Materials Research and Technology
Publication Date: 2024

Abstract:
In recent years, eutectic high entropy alloys have been found to have excellent mechanical properties and corrosion resistance; however, the simultaneous achievement of these mechanical properties and high- temperature oxidation resistance still constitutes a tough challenge. In this study, Ta and Cr elements are added to (FeCoNiNb)98Al2 high-entropy alloy to improve oxidation resistance, and the changes in microstructure and mechanical properties of (FeCoNiNb)98-2x(TaCr)xAl2 as well as the oxidation behaviors at 900 ◦C are sys­ tematically investigated. The results show that the addition of Ta and Cr elements promotes the increase and growth of the Laves phase in the alloy, which improves the oxidation resistance of the alloy and maintains the excellent mechanical properties of (FeCoNiNb)98Al2. The formation of CrTaO4, CrNbO4, and Cr2O3 composite oxide scales can not only prevent the diffusion of O into the alloy, but also inhibit the external diffusion of matrix elements. This result provide experimental data and theoretical support for the optimal design of the high- entropy alloy compositions to improve their antioxidant properties while maintaining excellent mechanical properties.

1. Introduction
Eutectic high entropy alloys (HEAs) are expected to be used in the 
petrochemical, aerospace and automotive industries due to their excel­
lent mechanical properties, corrosion resistance and good processability 
[1–4]. In recent years, a novel eutectic FeCoNiNb high-entropy alloy 
with typical FCC phase and lamellar Laves phase has been developed, 
which balances strength and hardness [5–7]. In addition, the excellent 
fluidity of these eutectic high-entropy alloys during casting effectively 
reduces the defects induced by segregation and shrinkage during so­
lidification [8]. However, the presence of the Laves phase as a diffusion 
channel for the internal diffusion of oxygen leads to a deterioration in 
the oxidation resistance of the alloy at high temperatures [9]. In addi­
tion, the Nb element has a high affinity for oxygen and is prone to form 
the porous and unprotective oxide Nb2O5 during oxidation [10–13]. All 
of these factors affect the high-temperature application of FeCoNiNb 
eutectic high-entropy alloys. Therefore, it is necessary to find a suitable 
way to improve the oxidation resistance of FeCoNiNb eutectic 
high-entropy alloys.
High-entropy alloys have a unique cocktail effect, and adjusting the 
composition of the alloys is an effective means of achieving excellent 
mechanical properties and high-temperature oxidation resistance [14]. 
Han et al. [15]investigated that the addition of 1.7 at.% Al to CoCrFe­
NiTa eutectic high entropy alloy induced the formation of ordered 
nanoprecipitates. The coherently ordered L12 precipitates were found to 
be thermodynamically stable against high-temperature annealing, 
which provided an effective strain hardening that improved the 
high-temperature performance of the alloy [16]. Therefore, the addition 
of small amounts of Al/Cr can promote the formation of ordered phases 
in eutectic high-entropy alloys, and the eutectic structure of the alloys 
will be more neatly aligned and denser to achieve higher strength. 
Eutectic HEAs show promise for high-temperature applications due to 
their unusually slow diffusion behavior, which could result in better 
creep resistance than commercial Fe–Cr-based stainless steels [17,18]. 
This makes eutectic HEAs potential candidates for use in components 
such as heat exchanger tubes in power plants operating around 900 ◦C

2. Experiment
The alloys were prepared from commercially pure Al, Co, Cr, Ni, Fe, 
Ti, and Si (99.9 wt%). The ingots with nominal compositions of 
(FeCoNiNb)98-2x(TaCr)xAl2 (x = 0, 6, 10, and 14) high-entropy alloys 
(marked as (TaCr)0, (TaCr)6, (TaCr)10, and (TaCr)14, respectively) were 
arc-melted in a vacuum arc furnace. Each sample was remelted at five 
times to ensure compositional homogeneity. Rectangular specimens (10 
× 10 × 3 mm) and cylindrical specimens (R4 × 6 mm) were cut from the 
alloy ingots for the oxidation and compression experiments, respec­
tively. All samples were ground with 1200# silicon carbide grit papers, 
washed in an ultrasonic alcohol bath, and dried immediately prior to 
testing. All samples were loaded into crucibles and placed in a muffle 
furnace (KLS-1500X–S). The oxidation experiments were carried out at 
900 ◦C in static air for 2, 4, 6,12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72 and 84 h respectively. 
The mass of the samples was recorded using an electronic balance 
(BS224S) to an accuracy of 0.01 mg. Compression tests were carried out 
on cylindrical specimens using an electronic universal testing machine 
(WDW-100) at a compression rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1. The composition and 
microstructure of the alloy matrix and oxide films were analyzed via X- 
ray diffraction (XRD, U1tima IV) and scanning electron microscope 
(SEM, EVO MA10) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectroscope 
(EDS). The volumetric fraction was determined by using Image-Pro Plus 
software to analyze the area occupied by the Laves phase within the 
matrix across different fields of view. To further understand the oxida­
tion mechanism, a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100) 
equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray microanalysis (EDX) system 
was used for fine characterization of the oxidation products. The TEM 
samples were prepared by the traditional cross-section sample prepa­
ration method, the TEM samples are glued with copper rings which 
finally thinned by a precision ion milling machine (GATAN 691), and 
specific details of TEM sample preparation can be found in ‘TEM spec­
imen preparation techniques’ [30].

3.2. Mechanical properties
Fig. 4 displays the engineering compression stress-strain curves of 
(FeCoNiNb)98-2x(TaCr)xAl2 high-entropy alloys at room temperature. 
The strength of the alloys decreases slightly with the addition of Cr and 
Ta, with the yield point approaching a stress of 1.5 GPa and a strain of 
8%. However, increasing the Cr/Ta content does not significantly alter 
the stress-strain behavior of the alloy. Research has found that eutectic 
alloys can withstand the greatest stress when the force is perpendicular 
to the direction of the eutectic arrangement, and the least stress when 
the force is parallel to the direction of the eutectic arrangement [7]. 
Therefore, the microstructural arrangement plays a more critical role in 
determining the compressive behavior of the alloy than the variations in 
Cr/Ta content. This suggests that the mechanical performance is more 
sensitive to changes in the structural features of the alloy rather than to 
the specific compositional adjustments of Cr/Ta. Although increasing 
the volume fraction of the Laves phase may adversely affect the me­
chanical properties, reducing the interlamellar spacing enhances these 
properties. The interplay between these opposing effects may account 
for the minimal overall changes observed in mechanical performance.
3.3. Oxidation behaviors
3.3.1. Oxidation kinetics
The mass changes of the (FeCoNiNb)98-2x(TaCr)xAl2 at 900 ◦C are 
shown in Fig. 5(a). The oxidation kinetics of each alloy essentially follow 
a parabolic law, with a sharp increase in weight in the early stages of 
oxidation followed by a levelling off. The oxidation resistance of the 
alloy was improved by the addition of the elements Ta and Cr, as shown 
in Fig. 5(b) which is the magnified image of (a). For a quantitative 
comparison of the rate constants of these alloys, the evolution of the 
mass gain over time was fitted using a power function, as follows (Eq .(1) 
[34,35]:

where ΔM represents the weight gain per unit area, Kp is the oxidation 
constant, t is the oxidation time, and n is a time exponent. The values of 
Kp and n for alloys with different Ta/Cr contents were obtained by fitting 
the experimental data (Table 2). The results show that the mass gain 
data for all samples are well fitted (correlation coefficient R2 > 0.99). 
The n values for all alloys fluctuated around 0.5. This suggests that the 
oxidation kinetics are approximately parabolic. However, the time ex­
ponents deviate from 0.5, which is typically attributed to defects in the 
oxide film, such as voids and cracks [36]. In the early stage of the alloy 
oxidation, the oxide film of the metal does not completely cover the 
surface of the metal sample, and the diffusion reaction at the alloy-gas 
interface is an important factor that mainly controls the alloy oxida­
tion rate. When the alloy is completely covered by the oxide film the 
interaction between the metal and the gas is blocked, and the diffusion 
reaction becomes the main control of the alloy oxidation rate [37]. 
Variations in Ta and Cr elemental content will result in different 
oxidation layers in the alloys and subsequent differences in oxygen 
barrier, which will show different oxidation mechanisms in the 
mid-oxidation stage. Comparing the oxidation rate constants of the al­
loys at 900 ◦C, it can be determined that (TaCr)0 has the worst oxidation 
resistance. The addition of Cr and Ta significantly improves the oxida­
tion resistance of the alloy, and the oxidation rate of the alloy at 900 ◦C 
is reduced by two orders of magnitude.
3.3.2. Microstructure evolution of the oxidation surface
Fig. 6 shows the XRD results of the alloys after oxidation at 900 ◦C for 
84 h. Four oxides are detected on the surface of the oxidized alloy (i.e., 
Fe4O3, NbCrO4, TaCrO4, and Cr2O3). Only Fe4O3 is detected on the 
oxidized surface of the (TaCr)0 alloy, which is attributed to the fact that 
iron ions diffuse faster than the cobalt and nickel elements [38], and that 
the alloy did not form any protective oxide layer during the oxidation 
process. The oxides of alloys containing the elements Ta and Cr are more 
complex than those of (TaCr)0 alloys, and it is the presence of these 
oxides that improves the oxidation resistance of the alloys. Ta2O5, 
Nb2O5, and Al2O3 are not detected from the XRD spectra because Ta2O5 
and Nb2O5 are consumed as intermediates in the generation of TaCrO4 
and NbCrO4.

insufficient to reach the critical values needed to form continuous Cr2O3 
and Ta2O5. As a result, Ta, Cr, and Nb eventually react with the oxygen 
diffusing into the matrix to form (Nb,Ta)CrO4.

preferentially oxidized, leading to delamination of the alloy during

formed on the surface of the alloy is not protective. However, the iron 
atoms continue to diffuse outwards, while the oxygen atoms continue to 
diffuse into the interior of the alloy. When Ta and Cr elements are added

4. Discussion
In the early stages of alloy oxidation, Fe, Ta, Cr and Nb elements are 
oxidized simultaneously, Fe atoms combine with oxygen to form 
oxidation protrusions, while Ta, Cr and Nb elements combine with ox­
ygen to form a composite oxide layer of (Ta,Nb)CrO4. Thermodynami­
cally, the free energies of Nb2O5 and Ta2O5 are actually lower than those 
of Cr2O3, and Nb and Ta should be oxidized before Cr. However, the XRD 
and EDS results show that almost no simple oxides of Nb and Ta are 
formed. Nb and Ta exist in the alloy mainly as intermetallics in the Laves 
phase, where bonding between Nb5+, Ta5+ and O2−is difficult. This 
leads to the simultaneous oxidation of Nb, Cr and Ta elements, while the 
simple oxides formed by the oxidation of Ta and Nb react with Cr2O3 to 
form complex oxides [46]. This results in the simultaneous oxidation of 
the elements Nb, Cr, and Ta, while at the same time the simple oxides 
formed by the oxidation of Ta and Nb react with Cr2O3 to form complex 
oxides [45]. The standard free energy data for the relevant oxides

The first principles calculation is used to calculate the formation 
energy (Ef) and binding energy (E0) of each oxide formed during the 
oxidation of the alloy. The crystal structure and formulae of the oxides 
are presented in the support material (Fig. S4). The negative formation 
energies of each oxide indicate their stability. The results of the calcu­
lation of the formation and binding energies of each oxide are shown in 
Fig. 10(a). The strength of the phase formation ability increases as the 
formation energy decreases, and the stability of the structure increases 
as the binding energy decreases. These calculated results are consistent 
with the thermodynamic data of the oxides shown in Fig. 10(b) [12]. 
This confirms the previously speculated oxidation mechanism of the 
alloy, i.e., after the addition of Ta, Cr elements, the alloy surface forms a 
protective oxide layer compared to the base alloy, and oxygen atoms 
diffuse slowly in this oxide layer, improving the oxidation resistance of 
the alloy. Based on the formation and binding energies of each oxide, it 
can be concluded that CrTaO4 and CrNbO4 are more stable and easier to 
form than Cr2O3 during the oxidation process. However, the generation 
of Nb2O5 and Ta2O5 is limited by the Laves phase, and there is less 
obvious competition between the formation of Ta2O5 and Nb2O5 and the 
formation of Cr2O3. The addition of Al element is not sufficient to 
compete with the simultaneous generation of Cr2O3, Nb2O5 and Ta2O5. 
As a result, the three oxides form a composite oxide layer with protective 
CrTaO4 and CrNbO4 in the subsequent reaction. This explains why 
Nb2O5 and Ta2O5 are not detected by the XRD test. The literature survey 
also indicates that certain Nb composite oxides possess excellent anti­
oxidant properties [48]. Additionally, the formation of CrNbO4 com­
posite oxides enhances the adhesion between the alloy matrix and the 
oxide layer, thereby improving the antioxidant properties the alloy [48].
Fig. 11 illustrates the mechanistic diagram of (TaCr)6, (TaCr)10 and 
(TaCr)14 high entropy alloys at 900 ◦C. The reason for the outermost 
layer of Fe4O3 oxidation is well understood and can be explained by the 
law of elemental diffusion coefficients. A layer of dense Cr2O3 appears 
on the surface of the alloy after a certain degree of oxidation, which 
greatly inhibits the diffusion of oxygen into the interior of the alloy [49]. 
The Nb/Ta-rich oxides inhibit the diffusion of chromium from the 
interior to the exterior of the alloy due to the weak affinity between Nb, 
Ta and Cr [45]. On the contrary, iron, cobalt and nickel have a strong 
affinity for Nb, Ta [45], and Nb, Ta-rich oxides can adsorb these three 
elements. Therefore, once Cr2O3 reaches a certain thickness, the growth 
stops. When chromium is depleted, iron diffuses from the Nb-rich, 
Ta-rich oxides and Cr2O3 oxide layer in the alloy to the alloy surface. 
Here, it reacts with oxygen to form a loose Fe3O4 oxide film [50]. In the 
later oxidation experiments, an abnormal increase in the oxidation rate 
of the alloy is observed with the addition of Ta and Cr elements. It is 
analyzed that during the oxidation process, the alloys form oxide layers 
with different thicknesses, coefficients of thermal expansion and ulti­
mate stress resistance. As the oxidation experiments progressed, the 
oxide on the surface of the alloy became more complex and thicker, 
resulting in the formation of microcracks. This resulted in an abnormal 
increase in the rate of oxidation of the alloy in the later stages of 
oxidation.
As shown in Fig. 8, internal oxidation occurred in all alloys. 
Furthermore, the oxidation process exhibits selectivity, with oxygen 
penetrating deeper into the alloy mainly through the Laves phase and 
reacting with Al elements to form Al2O3 [51], and with Nb and Cr ele­
ments in the Laves phase to form Nb2O5 and Ta2O5. In HEA, oxygen ion 
diffusion occurs mainly through a gap-filling mechanism. Similar to 
vacancy diffusion, when an oxygen atom moves to a low-energy site, it is 
"trapped" and unlikely to move away from that site [52]. Conversely, if 
the site is a high-energy site, the ion has a higher probability of returning 
to its original site, thus slowing down the diffusion process [53]. How­
ever, the diffusion of oxygen ions is much easier in the Laves phase than 
in the HEA. Diffusion of oxygen ions in the Laves phase leads to internal 
oxidation of the alloy and the formation of oxides of various sizes. The 
Laves phase exhibits increased enrichment of Ta and Cr, which enhances 
its affinity for oxygen and creates more voids to facilitate diffusion. In 
this system, the adsorption of oxygen in the Laves phase of the alloy 
leads to the formation of Al2O3 inside the alloy, which also promotes the 
formation of the (Nb,Ta)CrO4 composite oxide layer, thus improving the 
antioxidant property of the alloy. Moreover, due to the relatively low Al 
content in the alloy, the Al2O3 generated from the internal oxidation of 
the alloy does not have a major effect on the overall antioxidant property 
of the alloy.
5. Conclusion
In the present work, (FeCoNiNb)98-2x(TaCr)xAl2 high-entropy alloy 
with excellent mechanical properties and oxidation resistance is 
designed with elemental regulation by adding Ta and Cr elements. The 
results show that Ta and Cr elements improve the oxidation resistance of 
the alloy, which is two orders of magnitude higher than that of (FeCo­
NiNb)98Al2 eutectic high-entropy alloy. This improvement is attributed 
to the formation of a composite oxide layer consisting of CrTaO4, 
CrNbO4 and Cr2O3. The addition of Ta and Cr elements increases the 
volume fraction of the Laves phase in the (FeCoNiNb)98Al2 eutectic 
high-entropy alloy and reduces the interlayer spacing of the eutectic
