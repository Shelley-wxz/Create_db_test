# Enhanced degradation of tetracycline (TC) by FeCoNiCuAl high-entropy alloy powders through ball milling

Cite this: DOI: 10.1039/d6ra03618j

Hong Li,ᵃ Lijuan Zhou,ᵃ Hongjie Nie,ᵃ Junwei Gui,ᶜ Bowen Zhao *ᵇ and Zhengwang Zhuᵃ

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have garnered significant research interest owing to their exceptional catalytic properties in the degradation of organic pollutants. In this study, we report the synthesis of homogeneous FeCoNiCuAl HEA powders through an integrated single-roller melt-spinning and ball-milling approach. Compared to conventional gas-atomized counterparts, the ball-milled HEA powders demonstrate significantly enhanced catalytic performance in a Fenton-like system toward tetracycline (TC) degradation across varied temperature ranges. Notably, the BM-280 catalyst, processed at 280 rpm, achieves an outstanding TC removal efficiency of 94.8% within 60 min under H2O2 activation. The enhanced activity is attributed to the in situ generation of nanoscale active sites and a nanoporous structure, which synergistically promote reaction kinetics and interfacial mass transfer. These findings offer a novel and practicable strategy for designing advanced HEA-based advanced catalytic materials toward sustainable environmental purification.

# 1. Introduction

The escalating global consumption of antibiotics has posed unprecedented and intractable challenges to aquatic ecosystems.¹–⁴ Tetracycline (TC), as a representative refractory organic pollutant, persists and accumulates in water bodies, resulting in severe threats to environmental security and human health.⁵–⁸ This persistence can foster the proliferation of antibiotic-resistant genes and bacteria, thereby elevating the risk of a public health crisis. Among various remediation strategies, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have emerged as some of the most effective approaches, owing to their high efficiency and operational feasibility.⁹–¹¹ Consequently, developing highly efficient and stable catalytic materials for the advanced degradation of such persistent pharmaceutical contaminants in AOP systems represents an urgent and critical research imperative.

High entropy alloys (HEAs) have achieved controllable design of the electronic structure and atomic configuration on the catalyst surface through an almost infinite space of elemental combinations, effectively resolving the long-standing bottlenecks in catalysis related to activity, selectivity, and stability.¹²–¹⁶ Thus, the HEA materials based on their inherently disordered chemical structures have garnered intensive attention as an innovative catalyst in various applications, including the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and CO₂ reduction reaction (CO₂RR).¹⁷–²¹ Nevertheless, the exploration of HEAs in mental catalysis, particularly for organic pollutant remediation via Fenton-like reactions, receives increasing attention. For instance, the FeNiCoCrY HEA nanopowders synthesized via liquid-phase reduction exhibit sustained and efficient degradation performance of high-concentration Congo red and Cr(VI) in wastewater, owing to their high specific surface area.²² Dissimilarly, the high-entropy carbide nanoparticles prepared through combustion synthesis achieve approximately 65.5% tetracycline (TC) removal over ten hours via persulfate oxidation.²³ Moreover, the CoCrFeMnNi HEA nanoparticles supported on activated carbon demonstrate excellent degradation behavior of methylene blue without added peroxides, contributing to synergistic effects of solid-solution.²⁴ Despite these advances, the catalytic efficacy of such nanomaterials relies heavily on size-dependent effects, while the absence of scalable and stable synthesis methods continues to hinder their practical application in sustainable wastewater treatment.

Notoriously, the gas atomization and mechanical ball milling will offer a scalable and effective alternative for the engineering-scale manufacturing of HEA powders.²⁵,²⁶ In contrast, the rough surfaces of ball-milled powders provide more active sites than the smooth surfaces of gas-atomized powders, which may be beneficial for catalytic reactions.²⁷–²⁹ However, the direct ball milling of elemental powder blends frequently leads to issues such as severe compositional.

In summary, the present study proposes a novel integrated strategy combining single-roller melt-spinning with subsequent ball-milling to enable the scalable fabrication of HEA powders. Firstly, we established a convenient and efficient preparation route for FeCoNiCuAl HEA ribbons with homogeneous and tunable compositions via melt-spinning and followed by mechanical ball-milling to yield fine powders. # 2. Experimental

# 2.1. Preparation of HEA powders

The FeCoNiCuAl alloy ingots with a nominal equiatomic composition were manufactured by arc melting under a gettered argon atmosphere, using raw metallic elements each with an exceeding purity 99.9%. The brittle HEA ribbons were subsequently fabricated from the ingots via single roller spinning under argon atmosphere protection. The HEA powders were prepared by mechanically ball-milling the shredded alloy pieces for 90 min at two various rotational speeds of 200 rpm and 280 rpm, denoting as BM-200 and BM-280, respectively. The ball-to-material was controlled within a fixed weight ratio of 20:1 using steel balls for two different diameters of 8 mm and 6 mm. For comparative purposes, the commercially available spherical FeCoNiCuAl HEA powders produced by gas atomization were purchased from Beijing Yijin New Material Technology Co., Ltd, which were called as FP and GA-CP HEA powders.

# 2.2. Characterization techniques

The phases of the powders were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku D/max 2400, America) using Cu Kα radiation. The surface morphology, particle size and component distribution were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss SUPPA 55, Germany) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). The chemical states and electronic structures were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo ESCALAB 250XI, America) with an Al Kα radiation. The microstructural characteristics of the reacted catalysts were observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200, America) equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) for elemental mapping. The specific surface area of four powders was measured by Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET, Micromeritics APAP 2020, America) technique.

# 2.3. Degradation method of TC

The catalytic degradation performance of TC (Shanxi Tond Chemical CO., Ltd) was evaluated by the as-prepared NiCuAl HEA powders. The Fenton-like reactions were conducted in a batch reactor placed in a thermostatic water-bath shaker (SHA-BA) operating at 170 rpm and controlled temperatures of 25 °C, 35 °C and 45 °C. In a typical experiment, 0.5 g of catalyst, 40 mg per L TC and 8.16 mmol per L H₂O₂ were added to 60 mL of TC solution adjusted to pH 3. At 5 min intervals, approximately 2 mL of the reaction mixture was extracted and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 2 min, and the supernatant analyzed by UV-vis spectroscopy (Sunny Optical Technology UV-2000, China) and liquid chromatogram-mass spectrometer (LC-MS, Thermo Scientific-Ultimate 3000 HPLC, America). The absorption intensity at λTC = 358 nm was recorded to determine the residual TC concentration. The degradation efficiency (h) and reaction rate (k) were calculated according to the following equations for h = (1 − Cₜ/C₀) × 100% and k = ln(C₀/Cₜ)/t, where C₀ and Cₜ is the initial and residual TC concentrations, respectively. The active radicals were detected by electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (EPR, Bruker-EmxPlus, Germany) through DMPO as spin-trapping agent.

Emerging from Fig. 2(a–d), all four powders display diffraction peaks at identical positions, corresponding to a dual-phase solid-solution structure comprising both body-centered cubic (BCC) and face-centered cubic (FCC) phases, along with detectable B2-type ordered phase. Specifically, the BCC and B2 phases can be indexed to the (100), (110), (200), (211) and (220) crystal planes, whereas the FCC phase is assigned to the (111), (200), (220) and (311) crystal planes. Notably, the diffraction peaks of the BM-280 and BM-200 powders are considerably broader than those of the GA-FP and GA-CP powders, indicating lower crystallinity and finer coherent scattering domains owing to severe plastic deformation during intensive mechanical milling. Furthermore, the relative intensity of the FCC phase is more pronounced in the ball-milled powders, suggesting the distinction of phase distribution and microstructural evolution.

# 3.2. Catalytic performance of HEA powders

A comprehensive comparison of the degradation capabilities of four HEAs catalysts was evaluated by decomposing TC ants across a temperature range from 25 °C to 45 °C. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), it is clear that the ball-milled powders (BM-280 and BM-200) exhibit significantly superior degradation efficiency compared to their gas-atomized counterparts (GA-FP and GA-CP) under all temperature conditions. Notably, a slight difference appears in the TC removal between BM-200 and BM-280 catalysts. The TC removal efficiency of GA-FP is much higher than that of GA-CP, attributing to the fact that smaller dimensions offer a greater specific surface area. The degradation reaction follows the pseudo-first-order kinetics.

Fig. 3(b) further compares the apparent reaction rate constants of the four catalysts as a function of temperature. The degradation rates increase consistently with rising temperature, reflecting the thermally enhanced molecular diffusion and collision frequency that promote reaction kinetics. Among all samples, BM-280 catalysts demonstrate the relatively most outstanding degradation rate, highlighting its optimal catalytic activity.

To further evaluate the degradation capability of the BM-280 catalysts, the influences of H₂O₂ concentration, catalyst dosage and pH value on TC removal efficiency are systematically investigated. As shown in Fig. 3(c), increasing H₂O₂ concentration leads to a marked enhancement in TC degradation efficiency. In the absence of H₂O₂, the degradation efficiency reaches only 49.51% within 50 min, whereas it significantly increased to 92.69% upon the addition of 8.16 mol per L H₂O₂. This substantial improvement confirms that H₂O₂ serves as the primary source for generating reactive oxygen species (•OH and •O₂⁻), which are essential for driving the Fenton-like reaction.

# 4 Conclusion

To evaluating the catalytic performance of two types of HEA powders fabricated by the gas atomization and ball milling, the degradation efficiency and rate were systematically investigated towards degrading TC at variable temperature. Two types of HEA powders indicate excellent degradation efficiency, while the BM-280 powders exhibit preferable degradation capability than others. The enhanced degradation performance of BM-280 powders is attributed to the larger specific surface area as well as more exposed nanoscale active sites based on the surface structure changes caused during the reaction process. This research provides a new and feasible strategy for designing advanced materials toward efficient and sustainable degradation of pharmaceutical contaminants in wastewater.

# 3.4 Catalytic mechanism analysis

The possible catalytic mechanism for TC degradation in the HEA/H₂O₂ system can be proposed based on the above comprehensive analysis results. Initially, the intrinsic multicomponent synergy within the high-entropy solid solution provides a favorable electronic environment, facilitating the adsorption and activation of H₂O₂. During the critical reaction stage, both the metastable surface of the alloy and the in situ precipitated nanoscale composites act as highly active sites. These active sites efficiently catalyze the heterolytic cleavage of H₂O₂, generating a flux of potent reactive oxygen species, primarily hydroxyl radicals ($OH) and oxide radicals ($O₂−), which rapidly attack and fragment the TC molecules. Emerging from the analysis, the typical characteristic peaks of the corresponding $OH and $O₂− radicals were detected in the reaction system through EPR analysis.

Currently, the nanoporous structure drastically increases the specific surface area and enhances the accessibility of active sites, which can be conducive to the adsorption process of TC molecules. In addition, the residual stress is established within the alloy owing to severe plastic deformation and the atomic energy on the surface of the alloy is improved by mechanical ball milling. The change of residual stress and atomic energy may lower the activation barrier for surface redox reaction, thereby significantly accelerating the overall catalytic kinetics. Furthermore, the probable degradation pathways and some intermediates of TC by a series of reactions were identified by LC-MS analysis.

Firstly, the primitive organic molecular group for TC (m/z = 445) is fragmented into TC1 (m/z = 434) via demethylation and hydroxylation, TC2 (m/z = 429) via deamidation and TC3 (m/z = 396) via demethylation and dehydroxylation due to attacking of active radicals. Besides, TC4 (m/z = 418) is obtained by further demethylation and hydroxylation of TC1. TC2 may transform into TC5 (m/z = 401) through demethylation and bond cleavage reaction. Based on deep mineralization, these intermediates further are converted into secondary aromatic compounds by several chemical reactions referring to hydroxylation, oxidation, and ring opening and bond cleavage, viewing as TC6 (m/z = 286), TC7 (m/z = 164), TC8 (m/z = 122), TC9 (m/z = 149), TC10 (m/z = 108), TC11 (m/z = 181) and TC12 (m/z = 78), ultimately mineralized into environmentally friendly CO₂ and H₂O molecules during the degradation process.
